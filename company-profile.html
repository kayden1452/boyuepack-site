Company Profile — BOYUE PACK

Qingdao Boyue Trading Co., Ltd. is a flexible packaging supplier and supply-chain integrator in Qingdao, China. This page sets out how we operate, what we make, how the film is chosen, what we check, and what an order actually involves.

Company Overview

A packaging supplier, not a single factory

Qingdao Boyue Trading Co., Ltd., trading as BOYUE PACK, is a flexible packaging supplier based in Licang District, Qingdao, Shandong, China. We are a trading and supply-chain integration business rather than a single owner-operated plant. That distinction matters more than it first appears, because it is the reason we can quote a 500-piece digital trial in the same week as a multi-tonne gravure repeat order.

Our work sits between the buyer and the converter. We hold the specification, the artwork files, the approval record and the shipping documents in one place, and we choose the printing and pouch-making partner in Qingdao whose equipment and process best fits the job in hand. A short run with six artwork variants goes to a digital house running HP Indigo 200K. A repeat order on a stable design that ships by the container goes to a gravure converter where the cylinder cost is absorbed over volume. Both routes are quoted by the same person, on the same specification sheet, with the same inspection standard.

This model has one honest limitation, and we prefer to state it early: we do not claim to own a fully equipped printing, lamination and bag-making plant. What we own is the coordination, the specification discipline and the accountability. When a project needs a capability that no qualified partner in our network can hold to the required standard, we say so before an order is placed rather than after.

Operating Model

One contact, one specification, one approval record

Most packaging problems between a buyer and a Chinese supplier are not manufacturing problems. They are information problems: the specification drifts, the artwork version is unclear, the inspection criterion was never written down, and the person who agreed to it has moved on.

👤

A single point of contact

The person who quotes your job also owns the sample approval, the production follow-up and the shipping documents. You are not handed between a sales desk, an engineer and a logistics clerk who each hold a different version of the truth.

📐

Specification before price

We write the film structure, thickness, bag dimensions, print method, seal type and inspection criteria into one sheet before a price is given. A price without a structure is a guess, and it is usually the reason two suppliers appear to differ by 30 percent on what looks like the same bag.

✅

Approval kept on file

Sample sign-off, colour target, artwork version and any deviation agreed in writing are recorded against the order. On a repeat order six months later, the reference still exists, which is what keeps the second shipment matching the first.

🔁

Route chosen per job

The same product can be made two ways. We compare digital and gravure on total landed cost at your actual quantity, including cylinder or plate cost, rather than defaulting to whichever press is easier to schedule.

Product Portfolio

Format follows the product, not the catalogue

We do not sell a fixed list of stock bags. Each format below is a starting point that is then dimensioned, structured and printed for your product, fill weight and packing line.

🛍️

Stand-up pouches

The most widely used flexible retail format, and usually the first format a new brand adopts. A shaped base seal lets the pack stand on shelf, and the format accepts a zipper, a valve, a window, a spout or a hang hole without a redesign. Typical uses: coffee, snacks, dried fruit, pet treats, tea, powders. Typical structures: PET/PE, PET/VMPET/PE, PET/AL/PE, kraft/PE. Sizes are set by fill weight and the shelf space you are buying, not by a standard list.

📦

Flat bottom and eight-side seal bags

A box-style base gives the pack a rectangular footprint, which means better shelf presence, better stacking and a larger uninterrupted print panel than a stand-up pouch of the same volume. This is the format for premium retail: coffee in 250 g to 1 kg, pet food, grain and cereal, nuts and gift packs. It carries a zipper, a degassing valve, a window and a wide range of barrier structures.

🧴

Spouted pouches

A pouch with a fitted spout and cap, used wherever a liquid or semi-liquid has to be poured, squeezed or dosed. Spout diameter is chosen against viscosity: a narrow spout for low-viscosity liquids, a wider spout or an inner plug for sauces, gels and creams. The format serves sauces and dressings, liquid foods, detergents, personal care refills and large-format household packs.

✂️

Shaped and special-format bags

Die-cut outlines, handle bags, pillow packs, sachets and three-side seal bags. These are used where the pack itself has to perform a marketing function, or where the product dictates a shape the standard formats cannot hold — a bottle-shaped pack for a gift set, a handled bag for bulk retail, a small sachet for sampling.

🎞️

Printed roll film

Printed film supplied on roll for customers who fill in-house on a form-fill-seal or flow-wrap line. The film is specified for the filling machine, not only for the product: web width, treatment on the sealing surface, slip and coefficient of friction, and the position of the eye mark all matter to how the line runs. Micro-perforated and high-barrier variants are available.

🪶

Retort and high-barrier structures

For products that are sterilised in the pack or that must hold a long ambient shelf life, the structure has to survive heat, pressure and time as well as oxygen and moisture. Retort-grade film, foil lamination and cast polypropylene layers are specified against the actual sterilisation cycle, because a structure that works at 121 °C for 20 minutes is not automatically suitable for a longer or hotter process.

What we do not do: rigid plastic bottles, glass jars, metal cans, corrugated cartons and labels are outside our scope. Where a project needs those items alongside flexible packaging, we can coordinate them through separate partners, but we quote and warrant them separately rather than folding them into a packaging claim we cannot control.
Structure & Barrier

The film is the product's shelf life

Print gets the pack noticed; the structure decides whether the product is still good when it is opened. Almost every technical conversation we have with a buyer is about which of four threats the product has to survive, and for how long.

The four threats

Oxygen. Fats and oils oxidise, colours fade, vitamins degrade, and the reaction accelerates sharply with heat and light. High-fat products — nuts, coffee, fried snacks, cheese — need an oxygen barrier, and often a headspace that has been flushed with nitrogen at filling rather than only sealed.

Moisture. The direction matters as much as the amount. Biscuits and crackers must not gain moisture or they soften. Dried fruit must not lose it freely or it hardens and turns sticky. Fresh bread packed warm has to be allowed to release steam, which is the opposite requirement to a biscuit bag.

Light. Many of the same reactions that oxygen drives are accelerated by visible and ultraviolet light. A foil or metallised layer blocks light as well as oxygen; a clear pack on a bright shelf offers no protection at all, whatever the marketing says.

Mechanical stress. Sharp nut edges, hard biscuit corners and the load of a full 5-litre liquid pouch all attack the film. Puncture resistance and seal strength are specified against the real product and the real filling speed, not against a general rule of thumb.

A structure is therefore never chosen by name alone. The same three letters, PET/AL/PE, can be specified at different thicknesses and different sealants, and behave very differently at 250 g of coffee versus 5 kg of frozen product. This is why we ask for the product, the fill weight, the shelf-life target and the storage condition before proposing a laminate.

Common structures and what they buy you

  • PET / PE — economical print surface with a sealing layer; short shelf life, mild products
  • PET / VMPET / PE — metallised barrier against oxygen and light at moderate cost
  • PET / AL / PE — the full barrier: foil blocks oxygen, moisture and light together
  • PET / EVOH / PE — strong oxygen barrier in a lighter structure, with better recyclability than foil
  • MOPP / PE — moisture barrier with stiffness; the classic biscuit and snack construction
  • KRAFT / PE and KRAFT / AL / PE — paper appearance with a barrier behind it
  • PET / NY / PE — nylon layer for puncture and flex-crack resistance
  • Mono-material PE or PP — single-polymer structures for markets where recyclability is a requirement
On recyclability: mono-material PE and PP structures are available and are increasingly asked for in Europe, but they trade some barrier and some heat resistance against recyclability. We present that trade-off in numbers for your product rather than claiming a structure is both fully recyclable and equal in performance to a foil laminate, because it usually is not.
Printing

Digital for change, gravure for repeat

The choice between the two routes is an arithmetic question about your quantity and how often the design changes — not a question of which print is "better".

🖨️

HP Indigo 200K digital

Digital printing carries no cylinder cost, so the economics hold at short runs: a few hundred to a few thousand pieces per design. That makes it the right route for a first order before volume is proven, for a brand carrying several SKUs at modest quantities each, for seasonal or limited editions, and for artwork that is still being refined close to production. Colour consistency across a short run is managed with a digital colour target rather than a physical drawdown, and variable data — different flavour names, different origin text on the same bag — is practical in a way it is not on gravure.

The limitation is unit cost at high volume and a narrower choice of substrate widths. Above a certain annual quantity, digital stops being the cheap option.

🎨

Gravure

Gravure requires an engraved cylinder per colour, which is a one-off setup cost. Spread across a large repeat quantity, that setup becomes small per unit and the running cost is lower than digital. Gravure also holds dense solid colours, fine reverses and metallic inks with a consistency that is difficult to match, and it is the standard route for high-volume retail programmes where a design is stable for a year or more.

The trade-off is commitment: cylinders are cut for one design at one repeat length, and an artwork change after cylinders are made is a new cost. Gravure also carries a minimum film quantity per structure, which is why it is rarely the right answer for a first trial.

DecisionDigital when the design is new, the quantity is modest, or several SKUs ship together. Gravure when the design is settled and the quantity repeats.
Setup costDigital: none per design. Gravure: one cylinder per colour, charged once per design and repeat.
Unit cost behaviourDigital falls slowly with quantity. Gravure falls sharply, then flattens.
Artwork changesDigital: a new file. Gravure: new cylinders.
Best fitDigital for trials, multi-SKU ranges, seasonal packs. Gravure for retail programmes and container volumes.
Applications

Where our packaging is used

These are the categories we work in most often. Each has its own failure mode, and each has a dedicated page on this site that goes into the detail.

Coffee

Roasted coffee releases carbon dioxide for days after roasting and oxidises quickly afterwards. The pack therefore needs a one-way degassing valve, an oxygen barrier and usually a flat bottom or stand-up format with a zipper. Whole bean and ground coffee behave differently — ground coffee has far more surface area and loses aroma faster — so the barrier requirement is set against grind, roast level and expected time on shelf.

Pet food and treats

Dry kibble is a fat-and-moisture problem; soft treats are a moisture and aroma problem; supplements are a multi-SKU, low-volume problem. Pet packaging also carries a practical demand that food packaging does not: the bag is opened and closed many times over weeks, so zipper strength, hangability and the stiffness of a flat bottom base matter as much as barrier.

Snacks and confectionery

Fried and extruded snacks need an oil and oxygen barrier plus enough stiffness to protect the product from crushing. Candy and chocolate add a light and heat dimension. Metallised structures are common here, with foil reserved for longer shelf life or premium positioning.

Tea

Tea loses aroma, colour and freshness to oxygen, moisture and light, and the leaf is fragile enough that a stiff pack protects quality. Loose leaf, blends, pyramid and flat sachets and bulk packs each need different handling, and tea does not require a degassing valve — a common assumption that adds cost for no benefit.

Sauces and liquid foods

Spouted pouches replace glass jars and rigid bottles where weight, breakage and single-use portions matter. Oil-based sauces such as romesco, pesto and dressings require an oil-resistant inner layer; products that are hot-filled or pasteurised after packing require a heat-resistant structure and a seal that survives the cycle.

Bakery

Bakery is the category where the requirement splits in two directions. Fresh bread and hot-packed products need controlled venting so steam escapes instead of condensing into mould; biscuits, crackers and dry baked goods need moisture kept out entirely. Grease resistance and crush protection apply across the category.

Nuts and dried fruit

High-oil nuts are the hardest case in dry goods: they need an oxygen barrier, light protection and often nitrogen flushing, plus puncture resistance for hard edges. Dried fruit is primarily a moisture-stability problem, with blocking and sugar pick-up at the seal as practical concerns.

Personal care and household

Refill pouches for shampoo, hand soap, detergent and cleaning liquids. The film has to survive surfactants, solvents and acids, hold fragrance over the shelf life, and carry the fill weight without base-seal failure. Label requirements are set by the destination market and planned into the print area from the start.

Quality Control

What we actually check, and when

Quality in flexible packaging is decided at four points: incoming film, print, lamination, and the finished bag. A final inspection alone finds problems too late to fix them cheaply.

Incoming material. Film grade, thickness, width and treatment level are checked against the specification before the roll enters production. A sealing surface with the wrong treatment level produces bags that look perfect and leak, so this is verified rather than assumed.

Print. Colour is matched to the approved target — a physical drawdown on gravure, a digital colour reference on Indigo — with registration, ink adhesion and coverage checked across the run rather than only at the start. Barcode grade is checked where a barcode has to scan at retail: a beautiful pack that will not scan in a supermarket is a failed pack.

Lamination. Bond strength and the absence of bubbles or trapped solvent are checked on the laminated web. Delamination rarely appears at the factory; it appears three weeks later on a shelf, which is why bond strength is measured rather than eyeballed.

Finished bag. Seal strength and seal integrity, bag dimensions against the fill line, zipper function, valve function, spout fitment and cap tightness, tear-notch position and zipper opening force. For spouted and retort packs, a leak test is performed as well, because a pouch that fails under pressure fails in the customer's hands, not in ours.

Inspection is carried out against the criteria written on the specification sheet, and the results are recorded per lot. Where a customer has its own inspection protocol or a third-party inspection requirement, that protocol is agreed before production rather than negotiated after a dispute.

Typical inspection list

  • Film thickness, width and surface treatment
  • Print colour against approved target, registration, ink adhesion
  • Barcode scan grade where applicable
  • Lamination bond strength, no bubbles or solvent retention
  • Seal strength and seal integrity, including leak test on spouted and retort packs
  • Bag dimensions, base seal shape, gusset symmetry
  • Zipper, valve, spout and cap function
  • Tear notch position and opening force
  • Pack count, carton marking and shipping marks
Compliance & Documentation

Documents are confirmed per market, per order

Food-contact rules are not global. The same bag can be compliant in one market and non-compliant in another, which is why we treat documentation as a specification item rather than an afterthought.

For food and beverage packaging we work with converters who can supply the food-contact declarations applicable to the destination market — for example an EU declaration of compliance for plastics intended to contact food, or a US food-contact statement for the relevant material layers. What we do not do is describe a document as available before it has been confirmed for the specific structure and the specific destination, because the declaration follows the material, not the supplier's brochure.

A typical document set for an export order includes the technical data sheet for the structure, the food-contact declaration where applicable, the certificate of analysis if the customer requires one, the packing list and the commercial invoice. Additional items — a migration test report, a heavy-metal statement, a declaration on recycled content, a specific declaration for a retail programme's own audit — are quoted and scheduled separately, because each adds lead time.

Label content is a separate matter from material compliance. Ingredient declarations, allergen statements, nutrition tables, hazard symbols and the required local language are set by the destination country, and the printable area on the bag has to be planned around the text you must legally carry. We plan the layout for that footprint at the artwork stage rather than discovering at proof time that the mandatory text does not fit.

What to tell us at enquiry stage

  • Destination market, and whether it is retail or industrial use
  • Product type and whether it is a food, a cosmetic or a household chemical
  • Shelf life and storage condition (ambient, chilled, frozen)
  • Whether the product is hot-filled or sterilised in pack
  • Any document your own customer or auditor requires
  • Whether a specific certification is mandatory for your market
Process

From brief to shipment

A custom packaging order moves through eight stages. Knowing which stage you are at is usually the difference between a calm project and a late one.

1. BriefProduct, fill weight, shelf life, filling method, destination market, target quantity and required delivery date. This is where most projects are won or lost, because a brief missing the filling method cannot be structured correctly.
2. Structure proposalWe propose a laminate and thickness, bag format and dimensions, seal type, and the print route. You get the reasoning, not only the structure code, so the trade-offs are visible.
3. QuotationUnit price at the quoted quantity, setup and cylinder cost separated out, sample cost, packing and shipping terms stated explicitly. A price that hides setup is a price that changes later.
4. ArtworkFiles are checked for bleed, dieline fit, barcode placement, minimum text size and the mandatory label content for the destination market before anything is printed.
5. SampleA physical sample or a printed proof, depending on the route. Colour, seal, zipper, valve, spout and dimensions are approved in writing against the specification sheet.
6. ProductionPrinting, lamination, slitting and bag-making, with in-process checks at each stage. Lead time is stated from artwork approval, not from the enquiry date — the single most common source of misunderstanding in export packaging.
7. InspectionFinished-goods inspection against the agreed criteria, with photographs and the inspection record shared before dispatch.
8. ShipmentExport packing, carton marking, and the agreed trade term — EXW, FOB Qingdao, or CIF to your port. Documents are issued to match the term.

Two habits make this process reliable. First, everything agreed is written down, including deviations, because verbal approval is not approval. Second, the sample is treated as the contract: if the production run does not match the approved sample, the sample governs.

Trade Terms

Terms, ports and lead times

We ship from Qingdao, which is one of the busiest container ports in northern China and has direct sailings to Europe, North America, Southeast Asia, Australia and South America. Quotations are normally issued on FOB Qingdao terms; CIF and other terms can be quoted on request, with the freight and insurance component shown separately so you can compare against your own forwarder.

Flexible packaging is a light, high-volume product, which means freight is usually charged on volume rather than weight. A carton of finished pouches is bulky relative to its value, so packing density — how many pieces fit per carton and per pallet — genuinely affects the landed cost. We state the carton dimensions, pieces per carton and pallet configuration in the quotation for that reason.

Production lead time is quoted per order because it depends on the route, the structure and the season. Digital work moves faster than gravure, and gravure involves cylinder making before printing can start. Chinese New Year and the pre-holiday peak in Europe and North America are the two periods where schedules tighten, and we plan around them rather than promising dates the factories cannot hold.

Practical notes

  • Freight is volume-driven, so packing density is quoted
  • Lead time counted from artwork and sample approval

FAQ

Questions buyers ask before the first order

Are you a factory or a trading company?
We are a trading company with an integrated supply chain. Qingdao Boyue Trading Co., Ltd. holds the specification and the accountability, and works with qualified printing and pouch-making partners in Qingdao. We do not claim to own a fully equipped production plant, and we say so rather than implying otherwise.
What is the smallest order you can handle?
It depends on the format and the print route. Digital printing removes cylinder cost, which makes short runs viable; spouted pouches carry a higher practical minimum because each pouch needs a spout fitted and leak-tested. Tell us the format and quantity you have in mind and we will quote against it honestly, including where a smaller quantity stops being economical.
Can you supply a sample before a full order?
Yes. Depending on the route, a sample is either a plain structure sample to verify material and dimensions, or a printed proof to verify artwork and colour. Samples carry a cost and a lead time, which are stated in the quotation rather than absorbed silently into the unit price.
Do you publish a price list?
No, and a published list would be misleading. Flexible packaging price is a function of structure, thickness, format, print method, quantity and packing density. What we do instead is quote against a written specification so the price and the product are the same conversation.
How long does production take?
Lead time is quoted per order because it depends on the print route, the structure and the season. The clock starts at artwork and sample approval, not at the enquiry, which is why we state the date that way.
Can you make a recyclable pack?
Mono-material PE and PP structures are available where your market requires recyclability. They trade some barrier and heat resistance against recyclability, so we present the trade-off for your product instead of claiming equal performance to a foil laminate.
Who do I talk to during the order?
One person, from quotation through to shipment, at manager@boyuepackaging.com. That person holds the specification sheet, the approval record and the shipping documents, so you are not re-explaining your job to a new contact at every stage.
